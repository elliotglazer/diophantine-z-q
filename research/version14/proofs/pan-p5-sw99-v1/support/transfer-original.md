Write A=F[[T]], K=Frac(A), and r=rho(q)^o. Here F in A is the finite coefficient field; the totally real number field is denoted F_0 below. Pan's actual point is a characteristic-p nice prime, not the original characteristic-zero representation to which modularity is ultimately applied. ## Replacing the local ordinary hypothesis

The old hypothesis 6.2(vi) imposes upper triangular local representations whose character quotient has infinite order. In the proof leading to Lemma 6.9, its explicit use is to prove Lemma 6.1. It is not needed to identify Pan's local deformation rings here, and it must not be asserted for Pan's nice prime merely because it occurs in the older paper.

Here is an elementary replacement for the adjoint statement. Over a field of odd characteristic, the bilinear form tr(XY) on the three-dimensional space sl_2 is nondegenerate. In the basis H=diag(1,-1), E=e_12, F=e_21 its Gram matrix is [[2,0,0],[0,0,1],[0,1,0]], with determinant -2. If sl_2 has a proper invariant subspace, either it is a line or its orthogonal complement is a line. Let that line be spanned by X, so r(g)Xr(g)^{-1}=eta(g)X.

If det(X)=0, X is a nonzero nilpotent matrix. Its one-dimensional kernel is invariant under r, contradicting irreducibility. If det(X) is nonzero, determinant invariance gives eta(g)^2=1. If eta is trivial, X lies in the commutant. Absolute irreducibility then makes it scalar, and trace zero in odd characteristic makes it zero, again a contradiction. Otherwise ker(eta) preserves the two distinct eigenlines of X and the other coset interchanges them. This is exactly induction of a character from the quadratic subgroup, after adjoining the eigenvalues if necessary. Thus an absolutely irreducible, geometrically non-dihedral r has irreducible adjoint.

The required absolute irreducibility of r follows here from ordinary irreducibility and oddness, without an ordinary hypothesis at p. A complex conjugation is an involution with eigenvalues 1 and -1 in K. Any line invariant after scalar extension must be one of its two K-rational eigenlines. Thus it descends to K and contradicts irreducibility over K.

There is one coefficient-convention issue for independent review. Pan writes his inducing character as K-valued, while the invariant-line argument naturally produces it over a finite extension of K. In the standard geometric meaning of “dihedral,” this is the same case distinction after coefficient enlargement. Finite extension of A is faithfully flat, and any finite-length or zero conclusion descends. But if Pan's printed K-valued wording is interpreted literally to exclude nonsplit-torus induction, one must also verify that his nice-prime disjointness condition applies to the resulting quadratic subgroup. This proposal does not silently promote that convention check to an established hypothesis. The dihedral argument below explicitly uses that disjointness. This is the outstanding interpretation item C1.

## The subsequent representation lemmas

Set F'=F_0(det(r),zeta_(p^infinity)), and let F^+ be the fixed field in F' of a complex conjugation. Write H' and H^+ for the images of the corresponding groups in im(r). Oddness distinguishes complex conjugation from ker(r). The projections from Gal(E_infinity/F') and Gal(E_infinity/F^+) to im(r) are injective: an element of their kernels fixes E and F^+, and the possible remaining involution on F'/F^+ cannot fix det(r). Consequently the image notation used in Skinner–Wiles 6.9 is valid here. H' is pro-p, det(r) is trivial on H', and H^+/H' has order 2. The pro-p assertion uses the upper triangular residual extension with determinant killed, not a local p-adic triangularization.

The proofs of 6.2 and 6.3 then use the preceding adjoint irreducibility, odd complex conjugation and this pro-p structure. In particular, the infinite-order elements used later are derived, not assumed as a substitute local hypothesis. If every element of H' had finite-order eigenvalues, those eigenvalues would be 1. The trace computation in 6.3(i) with a nontrivial unipotent would force H' to preserve a line. For the other coset, finitely many possible traces combined with the powers of an infinite-order diagonal element would force a dihedral restriction. These contradict 6.2. The two residual eigenvalues in that coset are distinct, so Hensel's lemma puts the eigenvalues in A; squaring supplies the H' element. This recovers the exact form of 6.3 used in the measure argument.

For clarity, the finite-index use in 6.5 can be spelled out. Any finite-index normal subgroup J of H^+ contains a power of an element with distinct infinite-order eigenvalues. If r restricted to J were reducible, it would have at most two invariant lines, since these must be eigenlines of that element. Normality makes H^+ permute them. One line gives a reducible H^+ representation, and two interchanged lines give a dihedral one, both excluded by 6.2. If r restricted to J were dihedral, intersect the finitely many H^+-conjugates of its index-two inducing subgroup. This produces a finite-index normal subgroup on which r is reducible, contradicting the preceding argument. Applying the trace-pairing argument therefore gives adjoint irreducibility on J as well. This supplies the adjoint statement needed for the lattice-generation and invariant bounds in 6.5–6.6, rather than confusing irreducibility of r with irreducibility of ad^0(r).

The lattice bound itself is uniform in n. For each primitive vector x of the adjoint A-lattice, irreducibility over K gives a power T^a times the lattice inside A[J]x. Finite generation of a set of spanning translates makes this condition locally constant or locally bounded in x. The compact set of primitive vectors has a finite cover, hence a uniform exponent. The same lattice contains no nonzero invariant vector over K. This is the fixed-subgroup input to the bounded invariant terms in 6.6. It does not require a bound uniform over varying representations or arbitrary varying J.

## Reading the full 6.9 argument

The first Hochschild–Serre term in equation 6.12 is annihilated by the same fixed power that annihilates the invariant coefficient module. Only an annihilator, not a universal cardinality claim for arbitrary quotient groups, is required. Restriction through the order-two quotient and the cyclotomic twist identify the remaining term with the minus eigenspace in H^1(H',ad^0(r)/T^n). Since p is odd, taking that eigenspace is exact.

A cocycle corresponds to a fixed-determinant first-order deformation over A plus epsilon A/T^n. On H^+ minus H', the map in the printed proof is phi(sigma)=lim sigma^(1+p^m). The set H_M is defined by tr(r(sigma)) nonzero modulo T^M; it is not defined by tr(r(phi(sigma))). The infinite-order element of 6.3(ii) makes H_M nonempty. The bound on the fibers of phi modulo T^n is independent of n for fixed M. Hence the closure of phi(H_M) has positive Haar measure.

For the minus eigenspace cocycles, the trace perturbation vanishes on this set. The successive normalization on printed pages 96–97 uses an infinite-order diagonal element, then a positive-measure intersection and an element with a nonzero off-diagonal entry. The latter exists by finite-index irreducibility. The constants involve valuations of the fixed eigenvalue differences and fixed nonzero matrix entries. They are chosen from r and the positive-measure sets, independently of the cocycle and n. A further positive-measure intersection produces a subgroup of finite index on which a fixed T-power multiple of the cocycle vanishes. The finite-index invariant bound controls the inflation term. Together with 6.12 this yields a single T^N annihilating every finite-level H^1(Gamma,M/T^n M).

I checked all of this proof, including the definition of H_M and the last two basis normalizations, in the final page images. The local condition 6.2(vi) is not invoked anew in these steps. The local ramification types and local ordinary filtrations used in 6.7–6.8 are not inputs to 6.9's proof. This is a proof-dependency observation; it does not delete those conditions from unrelated conclusions of Section 6.

## From the finite levels to Pan's statement

Pan 4.3.5 uses the trace pairing followed by extraction of the coefficient of T^(n-1) to identify his finite dual module with M/T^n M. No inverse Tate twist or characteristic-zero dual is substituted.

For compact continuous cohomology, H^1(Gamma,M) is the inverse limit of these finite-level H^1 groups: the possible lim^1 of H^0 vanishes, since these H^0 groups are finite and their inverse system is Mittag–Leffler. The uniform T^N annihilator therefore annihilates H^1(Gamma,M). Its inflation into H^1(G_(F_0,P),M) is injective because the kernel defining Gamma acts trivially on M. The latter cohomology is the finitely generated A-module used explicitly by Pan 4.3.9. A submodule of a finitely generated module over A is finitely generated. Being also killed by T^N, it has finite length. This is the exact needed conclusion. Uniform annihilation alone, without this finite-generation step, would not prove finite length.

## Dihedral case

For the geometric dihedral branch, the quadratic field is L=F_0(bar-chi), with bar-chi quadratic, as in Pan 4.1.10. After a finite coefficient extension one may write the restriction as theta plus theta^{-1}, and theta reduces to 1. Its image is pro-p. Conjugation by the other coset acts by inversion; because p is odd, squaring is an automorphism of this pro-p abelian image. Thus the only nontrivial abelian quotient of im(r) is the quadratic quotient. The nice-prime condition L intersect F_0(zeta_p)=F_0 then gives E intersect F_0(zeta_(p^infinity))=F_0. Indeed a quadratic subextension of this cyclotomic tower is already in its first layer.

Consequently Gamma is the direct product of im(r) and the cyclotomic group. Its prime-to-p cyclotomic factor contains an element z whose action on M is the scalar omega(z) different from 1. This element is central. For a continuous 1-cocycle f, the cocycle relation for zg=gz gives `(omega(z)-1) f(g)=(g-1) f(z)`. Since omega(z)-1 is a unit, f is a coboundary. Hence H^1(Gamma,M)=0, including before coefficient extension by faithful-flat descent. This branch genuinely needs the nice-prime disjointness condition; irreducibility by itself is not a substitute.
