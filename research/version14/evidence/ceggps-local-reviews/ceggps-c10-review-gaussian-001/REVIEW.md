## Source check and deduction

1. **Residual domain.** The auxiliary residual representation is an input from the predecessor, not a new hypothesis imposed on the manuscript's representation. If a characteristic-zero lift acquires an invariant line after a finite coefficient extension, its intersection with a stable lattice is saturated. Reduction gives a proper nonzero line in the scalar-extended residual representation. Absolute residual irreducibility excludes this. This checks SC01 and ensures that the reducible characteristic-zero branches are unused throughout the restricted fixed-determinant space.

2. **The final Kisin statement.** I read final Asterisque 330 pages 520–527, including the fixed-determinant density statement, deformation groupoids, lattice adjustment, Proposition 2.8 and Corollaries 2.9–2.11. The source's old citation number `2.3.8` is not used as though it were a current locator. Final Corollary 2.11 gives Banach-lattice existence; its construction through 2.8–2.10 gives the stronger conclusion needed here. The lattice deforms the specified supersingular pi, so its reduction is exactly pi, not an unspecified representation with a related Galois image. This meets Image Proposition 5.56(iii)'s multiplicity-one requirement. The p=3 exception and reducible residual exclusions do not occur at p=5 with absolutely irreducible residual representation.

Kisin's argument changes a general unitary central character by a square of a character trivial residually before using fixed-determinant benign density. It returns by twisting. It does not require the original unitary character to be locally algebraic. The source's final pages 526–527 visibly print central character `det(V) epsilon^(-1)`. OCR that drops the minus sign must not define the application.

The asserted absolute topological irreducibility also follows from the exact residual lattice. A nonzero closed invariant subspace meets the lattice in a primitive vector after scaling. Its reduction is all of pi, since pi is absolutely irreducible. Iterative lifting modulo powers of the uniformizer and closedness fill the whole lattice. This argument works after finite coefficient extension. It verifies the Banach category and irreducibility hypotheses used next.

3. **Endomorphism ring and categorical fiber.** I read final Image Sections 5.7–5.8 and 6, especially Proposition 5.56, Propositions 6.1–6.3, Corollaries 6.5–6.8, and the invoked Theorem 4.36. In the supersingular case, the source sets the deformation determinant to `zeta epsilon`, obtains the ring surjection, and proves it is an isomorphism. Its proof uses the mod-p extension injection and the regular and Iwahori extension calculations. I checked the published supersingular-extension statements 10.7, 10.13 and 10.14 and Image 6.23; the endpoint weights are included at p=5. These are not characteristic-zero supercuspidal locally algebraic identifications.

Colmez VII.5.2's supersingular case is a mod-p splitting argument. I read the actual case on page 495: the Galois splitting gives a Borel splitting, and the cited restriction result gives the GL2 splitting. This is distinct from the later characteristic-zero identification whose replacement is being sought.

Image 6.8 supplies an anti-equivalence with finite-length modules over the completed local deformation ring. Hence the residue-field specialization of the projective generator is the unique irreducible object at that point in the supersingular block. The Kisin lattice belongs to that block and gives the same point. Lemma 5.53 identifies the specialized Galois module. This checks SC03–SC04. Equality of Colmez images without block membership, finite length, and the categorical equivalence would not prove this conclusion.

4. **The actual Colmez construction.** The additional SC05 argument is necessary. Final Corollary IV.4.11 alone states `D(Pi(D))=D`; it is not an inverse-reconstruction theorem for arbitrary Banach representations. I checked IV.2.13–14, IV.3.2–3, IV.4.7–9, I.3.11, II.1.14(iv), and II.2.11–12 against the stated reconstruction.

Every finite uniformizer quotient of the Kisin lattice is filtered by the same supersingular pi. It has neither finite subrepresentations nor finite quotients. IV.3.2(iii) therefore gives the full natural-module image of beta on Qp, while IV.4.7 gives the compatible GL2-equivariant map on P1 and its kernel. The kernel vanishes. The integral and Banach extensions in IV.3.3 and IV.4.9 use compatible coefficient-valued duals; the author's successor correctly avoids reversing an inverse system by naively dualizing its quotient maps. Compactness supplies compatible lifts at the integral level.

After inverting p, I.3.11(ii) identifies the **bounded** sharp and natural Qp modules for irreducible rank two. I checked the printed inequality: it is dimension at least two. II.1.14(iv) identifies the kernel of restriction from P1 with the unramified part, which vanishes here. Thus the beta image is exactly the natural P1 module. This is an equality of the indicated topological modules, not merely a dimension comparison.

The character is essential. IV.3.2 sets the target character to the inverse of the Banach central character, so here it is `psi^(-1)`. Before IV.2.14 the source defines the covariant Galois functor as the Tate dual of the representation attached to the contravariant D functor. Consequently `D(B)=D(V_P^*(epsilon))=D(V_P) tensor psi^(-1)` when `det(V_P)=psi epsilon`. The duality pairing II.2.11–12 and the rank-two construction identify the image with the continuous dual of the actual `Pi(D(V_P))`. Dualizing gives the required Banach identification. This completes the restricted endpoint; no appeal to Image 12.7 or the supercuspidal clause of Colmez VI.6.51 is needed.

Thus `V_P=r_C tensor epsilon`; the determinant changes from `psi epsilon` to `psi epsilon^(-1)`, while the Banach representation and its central character stay fixed.
